# Pillars of support

Power is not a thing a ruler has; it's a relationship. It lasts while enough people keep
cooperating: obeying, working, paying, enforcing, staying quiet. Remove enough
cooperation from the right places and even a powerful target can't act. The pillars are
the groups that supply it.

## Usual pillars

**A national government or ministry**

- Police and gendarmerie; the armed forces
- Civil servants and the officials who carry out policy
- The governing party, its MPs, its local branches
- Business groups and large employers
- Media owners and journalists
- Unions and professional bodies
- Religious bodies
- Universities, schools, students
- Local and regional government
- Foreign governments, the EU, international bodies
- Courts and the legal profession
- The public: voters, taxpayers, consumers

**A company**

- Its workers, especially those it can't easily replace
- Customers, especially the large ones
- Suppliers and contractors
- Investors, lenders, insurers
- The board and its non-executive members
- Regulators and licensing bodies
- Trade press and local press
- Partners whose name it borrows (sponsorships, certifications)

**A local body (council, school board, hospital trust)**

- Elected members, especially the swing votes
- Senior officers and staff
- The party branches the members answer to
- Local press and community groups
- Local employers and business associations
- Faith groups, residents' associations, sports clubs
- The higher authority that funds or oversees it

## The spectrum of allies

| Active allies | Passive allies | Neutral | Passive opposition | Active opposition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| agree and act | agree, don't act | undecided or unaware | disagree, don't act | disagree and act |

Move each group one step left. Most campaigns spend their time talking to active allies,
who are already there, and arguing with active opponents, who won't move. The ground is
the middle three columns.

## Signs a pillar is shifting

- People inside it start saying in private what the campaign says in public.
- Enforcement slows: orders carried out late, half-heartedly, or not at all.
- Its leaders go quiet where they used to defend the target.
- Its members turn up at campaign events as individuals, then as a group.
- It asks the target for something in return for its continued support.

When the security forces or the civil service shift, they rarely announce it; they slow
down. Watch for it, and never punish it with public scorn: a wavering officer the
campaign humiliates goes back to the target.

## Worked example

A campaign to stop a motorway extension through farmland. Target: the transport
minister, who signs the go-ahead.

- Pillars: the construction firm, its insurers and lenders, the regional council that
  must grant planning, local farmers' union, the minister's own party MPs in the
  affected seats, the regional paper, road-building workers.
- Spectrum: farmers' union is a passive ally (members oppose, leadership quiet). The
  three local MPs are passive opposition (party line, nervous about their seats). The
  insurers are neutral and unaware.
- Pick: the local MPs. The minister depends on them, the campaign can reach them through
  constituents, they have their own grievance (their seats), and asking the minister for
  a review costs them little.
- One step: from passive opposition to neutral, publicly calling for a review before
  the signature.
