# Preparing an action

## Roles

| Role | Job |
|---|---|
| Action lead | Makes the calls on the day: start, stop, change route, end |
| Marshals | Keep the group together, de-escalate, keep space from provocation, watch for people in trouble |
| Police liaison | One named person who talks to the police, so no one else has to |
| Filming team | Several people, different spots, recording continuously, uploading as they go |
| Legal observer | Notes arrests, names, times, badge numbers, where people are taken |
| Legal support | A lawyer or legal team on call; the number goes on every arm in marker |
| Medics | First aid, water, eye wash; a plan for the nearest hospital |
| Spokesperson | Briefed, with the holding statement; stays out of arrestable situations |
| Wellbeing | Checks on people after; organises support for the arrested and their families |

## Briefing, before every action

- What the action is for, and the demand.
- The nonviolent discipline, said out loud, and what to do if someone breaks it: step
  away, don't join in, tell a marshal, film it.
- What may happen (police lines, kettling, arrests, gas) and what to do.
- Buddy pairs: no one alone, everyone knows their buddy's name and emergency contact.
- The legal number, and the right to stay silent until a lawyer is present (check the
  local rules).
- Where to go if split up, and when the action ends.
- Who to call after.

## Nonviolent discipline

Agreed and published in advance, so the campaign can point to it:

- We don't use or threaten violence against people.
- We don't damage property (or: we decided in advance exactly what symbolic act, if
  any, and who carries it out).
- We don't carry weapons, or anything that could be called one.
- We don't respond to insults or provocation.
- We follow the marshals' instructions.
- We look after each other, and anyone hurt, including police.

Provocateurs and fringes: marshals keep the group together and physically separate from
anyone breaking the discipline; the filming team records them; the spokesperson
distances the campaign from them, once, clearly. Never chase or unmask anyone
yourselves.

## After an arrest

- The legal observer records name, time, place, officers, and where they're taken.
- Legal support is called at once.
- Someone waits at the station for release.
- The family is told by the campaign, not by the news.
- No one talks to press about a case without the arrested person's say and legal advice.
- Fines and legal costs: a solidarity fund, raised in public.

## Security

- Separate what's public (most of the campaign) from what's sensitive (an action's
  exact target and time, the chart of supporters).
- Sensitive plans: small group, encrypted messaging with disappearing messages, no
  details in open chats or on social media.
- Phones at an action: consider leaving personal phones at home or turning off
  biometric unlock; know what local police may demand.
- Assume large open meetings and groups are watched. That's fine if what happens there
  is safe to be seen, and the campaign is mostly public by design.
- Suspicion is a weapon too: a campaign that accuses its own members of being
  informers without evidence does the target's work.

## Documentation

- Film continuously, not just the dramatic moment: the moments before show who started
  what.
- Note the time and place; keep originals; upload copies off the phone during the action.
- Collect written accounts from witnesses the same day, signed and dated.
- Photograph injuries, with a scale and a date, and get medical records.
- One person keeps the evidence file, and it goes to the legal team first.
